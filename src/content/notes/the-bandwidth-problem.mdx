---
title: "The bandwidth problem"
summary: "AI keeps getting faster at thinking. We haven't gotten faster at telling it what we want."
category: "Human-computer interaction"
draft: true
---

Every generation of computing has widened the channel between a person and a machine: punch cards to keyboards, keyboards to pointers, pointers to touch, touch to speech. Each step reduced the translation work a human has to do before a machine can act.

Then the machines got smart, and the channel stopped widening.

A modern language model can absorb a paragraph of nuance in milliseconds, but producing that paragraph still costs me the same thing it cost in 1980: two hands on a keyboard and my full visual attention — or a voice interface that broadcasts my request to everyone in the room. The asymmetry is stark. Output bandwidth from machines has exploded; input bandwidth from humans is roughly flat.

This is why I find the "AI will just be an app" framing unconvincing. The limiting reagent in human–AI interaction isn't model capability anymore. It's the interface: how much intent can cross the boundary per second, at what cost in attention, privacy, and social friction.

Three properties seem non-negotiable for whatever comes next:

- **Private.** Speaking aloud is not acceptable in most of the places people actually are.
- **Hands-free and eyes-free.** The interface can't compete with the physical task in front of you.
- **Open-ended.** Fixed command sets don't compound. Language does.

Most current approaches satisfy two of the three. Getting all three at once is, I think, one of the most interesting unsolved problems in computing — and it's the problem Origin is aimed at.
